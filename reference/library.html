Operating notes

Eight things we believe, and why.

Open any note. These are the arguments we make internally before capital is committed, written out rather than summarized.

The organizations doing the most important work in the world are too often served by the worst software and the thinnest support. Nonprofits, associations, and the companies that serve them control real budgets and reach millions of people. Yet the tools built for them are chronically dated, under-resourced, and treated as an afterthought by the broader technology market.

The reason is structural rather than mysterious. Mainstream venture optimizes for markets where a single company can plausibly become enormous quickly. The social good economy is large in aggregate but fragmented by category, budget cycle, and buyer type, so it rarely produces the growth curve a fund needs to justify a position. The result is a market with real money, real users, and very little serious product investment.

That is precisely the shape of market a permanent-capital vehicle should want. We do not need a category to support a billion-dollar outcome in seven years. We need a specific unserved problem, a buyer we can name, and a business that can be profitable and durable. There are a lot of those here, and comparatively few people building for them.

What it means in practice: we will not build outside this economy, because outside it we would be an ordinary investor with no advantage.

Foundry for Good deploys capital from the retained profits of its own operating companies. There are no limited partners, no committed fund size, and no ten-year clock. That single fact changes almost every downstream decision.

  • The milestone changes. Ventures are built to be profitable rather than positioned for the next round. The measures that matter are customers and margin, not a markup on paper.
  • The exit question changes. A fund must eventually sell. We do not. The correct answer to "when do we sell" is frequently "we don't," which lets a company be built for durability rather than for a sale narrative.
  • The pace changes. No fundraising calendar means diligence can be slow at the front. Most theses die before capital is committed, which is the cheapest place to kill them.
  • The founder's incentive changes. Equity is a claim on distributions, a future sale, or continued compounding. A founder is not waiting for someone else's liquidity event to be paid for the work.

The honest limitation: permanent capital is finite capital. We can fund a small number of ventures well rather than a large number thinly, so we are far more selective about the market than a fund with a portfolio-construction mandate would be.

Our default is to buy into businesses that already have infrastructure and a leader with a record, rather than to start from a clean sheet of paper. Nexus Marketing and Double the Donation are the profile: real customers, a working motion, and someone accountable who has already been doing the job.

The reason is that the failure modes of a greenfield idea are mostly unknowable in advance, while the failure modes of an existing business are visible in its numbers. Diligence on a company with customers is a matter of reading what happened. Diligence on an idea is a matter of believing a story.

The same logic applies to people. We invest in the people as much as the business, which means the diligence on a venture lead is the same rigour we apply to any investment: what did you own, what did you decide, and what happened as a result.

What it means in practice: when we do build from scratch, we do it only after the customer, the contacts, and the path to first revenue are already concrete, and only with an operator whose record we can check.

A new company's slowest years are usually the ones spent earning an audience. Product can be copied, pricing can be matched, and capital is available to anyone with a good story. What does not transfer is a decade of ranked sector content, a community of practitioners, and a partner list built one relationship at a time.

Across the Foundry for Good network we own 17 domains and the sector relationships that come with them. Nexus Marketing has ranked nonprofit-sector content for over a decade. NXUnite convenes thousands of nonprofit professionals. Double the Donation sits inside tens of thousands of donation flows. A new venture launches into that footprint, warmed by a 500+ partner network, instead of starting cold.

This is why the studio's advantage is concentrated rather than general. The distribution is specific to one economy, and it is worth a great deal inside it and nothing outside it.

Most people who want to start something never do, because starting means giving up a salary and self-funding through the most fragile stage of a business. That filter does not select for the best operators. It selects for the operators who can afford eighteen months without income.

Paying a base salary from day one and funding roughly two years of runway removes that filter and replaces it with a better one: can you run a P&L, and have you done something comparable before. It widens the pool to experienced operators with families and obligations, who are frequently the people most capable of running a company well.

It also changes what we can ask for. A funded, salaried founding team is expected to be full-time, accountable, and available for a bi-weekly operating review. That expectation is only fair because the risk was moved off the founder's personal balance sheet and onto ours.

Founding teams own 15–45% of the company they build; the studio holds the majority. That split is a straightforward consequence of who is carrying what.

The studio puts in the capital, the two-year runway, and full access to a fourteen-year operating platform: shared engineering, design, growth, finance, recruiting, distribution, and brand. It also carries the loss if the venture does not find its market, without leaving the founding team personally in debt.

The founding team puts in the thing that cannot be bought: full-time accountability for the outcome. That is worth a real, meaningful stake, sized to the role, the contribution, and the stage they join. It is ownership, not options theater.

Where this is the wrong deal: if you want majority control from day one, or your plan depends on raising a priced round on outside capital, another model will serve you better and we will say so early.

A studio that only publishes its wins is not worth reading. Three things we have had to correct:

  • Validating the market before validating the operator. Early on it was tempting to run both searches in parallel. It does not work: the operator profile is a function of the thesis, and defining it first produces a worse match. Diligence now finishes before the search starts.
  • Underestimating what an acquisition needs after close. Tradewing required a full platform rebrand, three new departments, and a rebuilt product cycle. Capital was the smallest part of that. We now plan the first year of operating work before we agree a price.
  • Treating shared services as goodwill. Shared functions only work when they are real capabilities with owners and capacity, not favours between busy people. Formalizing them was the difference between a group of companies and a platform.

The portfolio serves the U.S. social good economy, and it will continue to. The investment and operating bench behind it is being built in the Philippines, and that is a deliberate decision rather than a cost one.

The studio needs people who can do three things: run real diligence, sit credibly in an operating review with a U.S. company's leadership, and eventually take a venture lead seat. The Philippines has a deep pool of finance and operating talent with exactly that profile, and comparatively few venture platforms competing for it. A studio bench there is a first-choice seat rather than a fallback one.

Practically, it also means the studio is not dependent on two partners' calendars. Adam and Rafi cannot stay hands-on with every company as the portfolio grows. A permanent team that moves alongside them is what makes the next seven companies possible.
